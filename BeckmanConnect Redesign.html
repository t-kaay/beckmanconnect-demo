Case study · BeckmanConnect Remote Support · Beckman Coulter Life Sciences

Making remote service something labs say yes to

BeckmanConnect lets our support specialists fix most instrument faults without anyone getting in a car. Most customers still ask for someone onsite. This case study is about why, and the redesign I proposed to close that gap for commercial, mid-size and small laboratories.

The problem

Demand is growing faster than the engineering team

Our field engineering team is small compared with the service demand it carries. Every onsite visit costs travel time, and while a customer waits for an engineer, their instrument is down and their staff are blocked.

BeckmanConnect Remote Support already offers a hybrid model: specialists diagnose and fix remotely first, and when a part is needed the engineer arrives with a plan, tools and parts. It shortens downtime, speeds up production for customers and lets each engineer resolve more cases. The capability exists. Adoption does not: 76% of customers prefer onsite maintenance, so most cases still go into the travel queue.

That makes this a design problem as much as a technical one. Customers are not rejecting a feature. They are declining to let someone they cannot see into a system they are accountable for.

The loop we are stuck in

Breaking any one link helps, but the first link, trust, is the only one design can move directly. The others follow from it.

Where trust breaks today · evidence from public documentation

Six points where customers stall before the first session

Public reviews of BeckmanConnect are scarce, so I went to Beckman's own FAQ and security documents and cross-checked each friction point against our interviews. Most of the protection customers ask for already exists. They just can't see it.

Security

"It runs on TeamViewer"

Remote desktop sharing uses a customized TeamViewer client. In June 2024 TeamViewer disclosed a breach of its corporate IT network, attributed to APT29. Beckman published a notice that its own servers weren't affected and its logs showed no unauthorized access.

What it means: IT hears "TeamViewer" and stops the conversation before they see Beckman's own safeguards.

IT setup

The first session needs IT

Setup needs Windows admin rights, an IT requirements review, firewall rules for Beckman, Azure and TeamViewer servers on port 443, removing any existing TeamViewer, and on some systems antivirus updates or a certificate utility.

What it means: a lab manager can't say yes alone.

Trust

Controls exist but are hidden

Every session needs a one-time password and on-screen approval, and Beckman staff sign in with two-factor authentication. But labs can't see who is on the remote access list, and session logs, kept for a year, are available only by emailing support.

What it means: the lab has to take protection on faith.

Privacy

PHI rules aren't shown when it matters

File transfer is disabled on instruments that can hold personal health information, such as Navios EX, DxFLEX and AQUIOS CL. Customers aren't shown this when they approve a session.

What it means: a strong safeguard earns no trust because nobody sees it.

Cost

Eligibility is a surprise

Remote support is available only during the warranty period or on a service plan, and no pricing is published.

What it means: small labs can't tell whether trying remote will cost them.

Communication

Status lives in Windows pop-ups

Notices about configuration problems, updates and contract expiry arrive as Windows notifications, at most once a day per type, and instruments that were offline can miss them.

What it means: problems that block remote help surface only when help is needed.

Research

Four reasons customers say no

I ran interviews and focus groups with the people who influence a service decision: lab managers, bench scientists, IT and security administrators, QA and compliance leads, procurement, and our own field engineers. Four themes came up in every group, and each one lines up with a friction point in the documentation above. They overlap, but each points at a different part of the experience.

T

Trust: "Who is on the other end, and what are they doing?"

Underlying concern

Remote work is invisible. Onsite, a lab manager can stand next to the engineer. Remotely, they lose sight of who is connected and what is being changed.

Design implication

Make the engineer identifiable and every action visible as it happens, with the customer able to pause or end the session at any point.

P

Data privacy: "Will they see our samples and results?"

Underlying concern

Instruments sit next to sensitive data: patient-derived samples, proprietary assay results, unpublished research. Customers assume remote access means access to all of it.

Design implication

Separate instrument health data from experiment data by default, and show that boundary in plain language before and during every session.

S

Security: "Are we opening a door into our network?"

Underlying concern

IT teams treat any persistent remote connection as an attack surface. For regulated labs, an unreviewed connection can also break validation and audit requirements.

Design implication

No always-on access. Sessions are requested, approved, time-limited and recorded, and IT sets the policy once instead of reviewing every case.

C

Cost: "Am I paying the same for less?"

Underlying concern

Customers are not sure how remote service is priced, and some see it as a cheaper service sold at full price. Smaller labs fear surprise charges if a remote fix turns into a visit.

Design implication

Show the price, the expected time to fix and the downtime of each option side by side before the customer chooses, and credit remote time if a visit is still needed.

Theme statements are synthesized from sessions. In the interview, swap in two or three verbatim quotes and the participant counts from your own research notes.

Customer segments

Same four concerns, different weight in each lab

The themes were shared across customers, but who decides and what blocks a "yes" changed with the size and regulatory load of the lab. One flow had to flex for all three.

Concern weightings show relative emphasis observed across sessions, not survey scores.

Reframe
Customers don't distrust remote service. They distrust access they can't see, limit or stop. So the goal shifted from "convince customers remote is safe" to "give customers the controls they have when an engineer is standing in the room."

How might we let a lab manager approve remote help in under two minutes, with the confidence they would have watching an engineer at the bench?

How might we let IT and QA set the rules once, so they are not a bottleneck on every service call?

How might we make the cheaper, faster option feel like the better option, not the lesser one?

Design principles

Visible

Every action the engineer takes appears in the customer's feed as it happens.

In your hands

The customer approves, pauses and ends. Changes to settings need a second tap.

Scoped

Least access by default. Instrument health in, experiment data out. Every session expires.

Provable

Each session ends with a signed record that QA can file without rework.

Priced upfront

Cost, time to fix and downtime are shown for every option before anyone commits.

Security & data model

Most of the protection exists. Customers can't see it.

BeckmanConnect's architecture is already strong: outbound-only connections, per-session passwords, two-factor sign-in for Beckman staff, approval prompts and logs. The redesign doesn't start from zero. It moves each safeguard from a PDF on the website into the moment a lab decides.

Cost

Make the better option look better

Research showed cost worries were mostly about uncertainty, not price. The service selection screen compares options on the three things a lab manager is judged on: when the instrument is back, what it costs, and how much staff time it takes.

Transparency

One screen, three options

Remote, hybrid and onsite are priced and timed side by side, with the recommended option explained in one sentence.

Risk reversal

Remote time is credited

If a remote session ends in a visit, the remote time is credited against it. Trying remote first never costs more.

Eligibility

Coverage shown before booking

Remote support today requires warranty or a service plan. The app says whether an instrument is covered, and what a session costs if not, before the lab chooses.

To validate

Pricing is a hypothesis

Credits and introductory offers need commercial and finance sign-off. I proposed testing two framings in the pilot before committing.

Rollout

Start where a yes is easiest, then earn the hardest one

Phase 1 · pilot

Mid-size labs

Enough volume to learn quickly, some IT support, and less change-control overhead than regulated sites.

  • Usability tests on the consent screen with lab managers and IT
  • Ship the IT setup kit and connection check
  • Instrument the trust funnel from day one
Phase 2 · expand

Small labs

Highest cost sensitivity and no IT team, so plain language and one-tap approval matter most.

  • First session included
  • Guided setup the lab can finish without IT
  • Engineer intro call before the first session
Phase 3 · regulated

Commercial & GxP labs

The largest accounts, and the longest sales and validation cycles. Arrive with evidence from phases 1 and 2.

  • Validation package and security summary for IT and QA
  • Two-person approval and Part 11 signatures
  • Policy console for site-wide rules
Trade-offs

Decisions I would defend, and what I would watch

Approval on every session

Adds a step that slows the fastest cases. I kept it because it is the trust mechanism. IT can pre-approve read-only diagnostics, off by default.

Setting changes need a second tap

Engineers worried this would slow them. In practice it replaces the "may I?" an engineer asks at the bench anyway, and it makes the audit trail meaningful.

Showing the onsite option at all

Hiding it would raise remote numbers short term and cost trust long term. Customers choose; we recommend and explain.

Gateway hardware

A lab gateway adds install effort. It is what makes data separation real rather than a policy promise, so it stays, with a software option to explore for small labs.
